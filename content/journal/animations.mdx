Motion on the web is usually treated as ornament. Something to add at the end,
once the "real" work is done. That framing is backwards.

Animation is how a page communicates _change_. When a menu opens, when a form
validates, when a section enters the viewport — motion tells the user what just
happened, and what to expect next.

## What good motion does

- **Explains state.** A loading spinner is not just a pretty graphic. It is the
  honest answer to "did my click register?"
- **Directs attention.** A slide-in error message pulls the eye to the field
  that needs fixing. A silent red border does not.
- **Reduces cognitive load.** When elements move from where they were to where
  they are, the user's brain does not have to re-scan the whole page.
- **Rewards curiosity.** A hover state, a lens reveal, a scroll-triggered reveal
  — small moments of surprise that make a site feel cared for.

## What bad motion does

- **Steals attention.** Animation that plays on every scroll, that never stops,
  that fights for the eye.
- **Blocks interaction.** Loading screens that take two seconds to fade out so
  the user can click.
- **Ignores the device.** A blurred, 60fps, GPU-heavy scene on a phone with a
  three-year-old chip.
- **Ignores the user.** The same intense animation plays for someone who has
  `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce` set on their OS.

The difference between good and bad motion is not aesthetic. It is intent.

## How I build motion

Three rules, always:

1. **Only animate `transform` and `opacity`.** Everything else causes layout or
   paint work that the browser cannot offload to the GPU.
2. **Respect `prefers-reduced-motion`.** Not as an afterthought. As the default,
   then opt _into_ motion for users who want it.
3. **Tie motion to scroll.** Scrolling is the most direct gesture a user has. A
   page that responds to it feels alive without demanding attention.

The techniques are not fancy. Framer Motion, a scroll progress value, and a
handful of transforms. What makes them work is restraint.

## The point

Animation is not a coat of paint. It is part of the structure of an interface.
It is the difference between a page that responds to you and a page that just
sits there.

Do it deliberately. Skip it on low-end devices. Give the user control. Then let
it do its work.
